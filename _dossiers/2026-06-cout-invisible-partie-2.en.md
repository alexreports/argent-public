SAAQclic is the fullest documentation of Quebec's public IT failure pattern. The Auditor General audited it, the Gallant Commission examined it for 75 days with more than 120 witnesses, and the province's anti-corruption unit is now investigating it criminally. All five recurring causes identified in [Part One](/en/dossiers/cout-invisible-partie-1/) — vendor dependence, anti-competitive procurement, neutralized warnings, late-arriving oversight, and absent consequences — are visible here in a single project whose cost grew from $458 million to roughly $1.1 billion.

## The questions this case answers

What did decision-makers know, and when. Why did a written warning from inside the agency go nowhere. And why did the cost of a single project run past a billion dollars before anyone with the authority to stop it stepped in. SAAQclic answers each of these on the public record.

## What SAAQclic was

CASA, the Carrefour des services d'affaires, was the program launched in 2014 to modernize the technology of the Société de l'assurance automobile du Québec (SAAQ). SAAQclic was its public-facing portal. When the second delivery launched in February 2023, it failed. Systems crashed, transactions stalled, and service centres filled with long queues. That visible breakdown eventually drew the audits and the inquiry. What those reviews found was that the failure had been years in the making.

## The five failures, in one project

**1. Dependence on outside vendors.** The contract was won by a consortium known as the Alliance, made up of LGS (owned by IBM) and the German software publisher SAP. The tender required the work to be carried out in Quebec, yet the consortium acknowledged before the commission that building the system in Quebec City was not feasible given the available workforce. A modernization meant to strengthen a public agency's own systems was, from the outset, built on capacity the province did not hold.

**2. Procurement that removed competition.** According to the Auditor General's report, SAP was the only supplier meaningfully involved in the bid for the platform. Testimony showed that the SAAQ's head of information technology, Karl Malenfant, had manoeuvred with outside consultants to ensure SAP took part — communicating through his personal email by what one consultant labelled the "unofficial channels" — and that he had obtained a competitor's sealed bid that was meant to remain locked away.

**3. Warnings absorbed rather than acted on.** In March 2017, a former manager within the information-technology division wrote to two ministers — the President of the Treasury Board and the Minister of Transport — raising what she called major issues at the agency, including contract management and conflicts of interest. One minister later noted the letter had come in the wake of the Charbonneau Commission and that the matter was to be checked with the anti-corruption unit, but said he did not know what follow-up occurred. His successor said he had never personally heard of the letter. The warning named the right problems years before the failure and produced no visible action.

**4. Oversight that arrives after the money is spent.** Concerns about the project existed early, and the anti-corruption unit had looked at it before the public mandate to do so. Yet formal reckoning came only after the damage was done. The Auditor General's report landed in February 2025 — two years after the launch failure and roughly eight years after the contract was signed. The commission reported in February 2026. By the time the oversight architecture engaged fully, the cost was fixed and most of those responsible had moved on.

**5. No personal consequence.** As the file became public, coverage noted that most principal actors in the affair had retired. A criminal investigation by the anti-corruption unit is ongoing with several suspects identified, and no charges have been laid. The project failed without any individual having borne a cost at the time it mattered.

## What the commission concluded

On February 16, 2026, the Commission Gallant delivered a 586-page final report with twelve findings and twenty-six recommendations. Its central conclusion: the SAAQ had misled Parliament, ministers, and their offices for the better part of a decade about the true state and cost of the project. The program was too large, too ambitious, and meant to be built too quickly. Tying it to a single supplier opened the door to overruns.

On the money, the figures should be stated plainly because they are often confused. The 2017 contract was worth $458 million over ten years. The Auditor General found that costs rose by nearly $500 million without that increase being clearly communicated to decision-makers. The revised cost reached roughly $1.1 billion. The commission itself noted there is no single agreed method for calculating the program's full cost — which is itself part of the problem.

The commission also addressed political responsibility. It concluded that the government was not directly responsible, having made its decisions on the basis of inaccurate information supplied to it. Among its recommendations: clean up the management of technology contracts and improve the transparency of accountability across the state — the same repairs named after Charbonneau, and the same the AMP returned to in May 2026.

## The public cost

Citizens absorbed the launch failure directly — through crashes, stalled transactions, and long waits for a basic public service. Taxpayers absorbed an overrun that decision-makers say they were not told about. And the public absorbed a slower cost: the erosion of trust that follows when an agency is found to have misled the legislature for years and when, by the time the truth is established, the people responsible are gone.

## What must change

The reforms are not new, and that is the point. The 2017 warning invoked Charbonneau by name. The Auditor General named the gaps in 2025. The commission named them again in 2026, and the AMP named them across eleven projects weeks later. They converge on the same repairs: protect those who warn from inside, preserve genuine competition in procurement, rebuild the state's own capacity, and ensure accountability that does not vanish when a project fails. The authority to act sits with the government and the Treasury Board. The recommendations are on the record. What is missing is implementation.

## Where this sits in the series

SAAQclic is the proven case — the one audited, investigated, and reported in depth. It establishes that the pattern is real and that the system's own bodies have documented it.
