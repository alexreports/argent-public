Quebec's large public technology projects fail repeatedly, in the same patterns, across different governments and different parties. Two independent oversight bodies reached the same structural conclusion in the space of a single year: the Autorité des marchés publics (AMP), which reviewed eleven projects, and the Commission Gallant, which spent 75 days examining one. Their written findings now make the case that the pattern is systemic — not a claim by critics, but the documented verdict of the province's own institutions.

## The question this series asks

When a public technology project runs hundreds of millions of dollars over budget, arrives years late, or is quietly rebuilt at a price the public cannot find, two questions follow. The first is what went wrong. The second, and the harder one, is why the same thing keeps going wrong, and why the bodies created to prevent it keep arriving after the money is gone.

This series puts those questions on the public record. Part One sets out the pattern and the evidence that the system itself has now named it. [Part Two](/en/dossiers/cout-invisible-partie-2/) examines SAAQclic, the failure Quebec has already studied in depth. [Part Three](/en/dossiers/cout-invisible-partie-3/) examines Votre Santé, a newer case where the failure is not only a cost overrun but a cost placed beyond public view.

## The pattern

Quebec's large public IT failures share five recurring causes that appear regardless of who is in power:

- **Vendor dependence** that hollows out the state's own capacity to build and manage technology
- **Anti-competitive procurement** that binds the state to a single supplier with few ways out
- **Neutralized warnings** — internal alerts that are absorbed rather than acted on
- **Late-arriving oversight** — watchdog bodies that document failures after the money is spent
- **No personal consequence** when a project fails

The shape is consistent. A worthy public objective — a modern licensing system or a digital health record — is pursued through a mechanism that is technical, spread across several agencies, and shielded from ordinary view. The people paying cannot readily see what their money is buying. By the time anyone with authority looks closely, the cost is fixed and the responsibility has dispersed.

## How Quebec built an oversight system that arrives too late

The Charbonneau Commission delivered its final report on corruption in public contracting in 2015. Among its central recommendations: stronger independent oversight of procurement and real protection for internal whistleblowers.

In 2017 Quebec created the Autorité des marchés publics (AMP) as a neutral, independent body charged with watching over public contracts. The architecture was built. What the cases since then show is that it was built downstream of the decisions that matter — able to document failures after they occur rather than prevent them before the money is committed.

## The watchdog's own verdict

<div class="pullquote pullquote--verdict">
<p>The AMP issued twenty findings and nine recommendations, identifying significant gaps in governance, project management, oversight, expertise, and accountability.</p>
<span class="pullquote__source">— Autorité des marchés publics, May 14, 2026</span>
</div>

On May 14, 2026, the AMP delivered the clearest statement yet that the problem is structural. At the request of the President of the Treasury Board, it reviewed how public bodies award and run the contracts behind their digital-transformation projects, examining eleven projects carried out between 2010 and 2025 and drawing on academic studies, public audits, and meetings with technology firms and government bodies.

Its conclusion was not narrow. The AMP found that governance gaps contribute directly to overruns in cost, schedule, and scope, and that they expose some projects to a high risk of outright failure. The Authority was explicit: the twenty findings describe interrelated problems that cannot be corrected by isolated or one-off measures.

This matters for one reason above all. The body created after Charbonneau to be the watchdog has now made the case itself, in writing, and addressed it to the government.

The AMP's first recommendation calls for the central governance of public digital projects to be repositioned within the Treasury Board secretariat, and for the expertise of the state's own digital ministry to be strengthened. Its chief executive noted that the government already holds internal technology expertise, but that it must be mobilized far more effectively if public digital projects are to succeed.

## A second verdict in the same year

The AMP report did not stand alone. Three months earlier, on February 16, 2026, the Commission Gallant delivered its final report on SAAQclic, the failed modernization of the province's automobile insurance services.

After 75 days of hearings and more than 120 witnesses, Judge Denis Gallant produced a 586-page report with twelve findings and twenty-six recommendations. The commission found that the SAAQ had misled Parliament, ministers, and their offices for the better part of a decade about the true state and cost of the project. It found that the program had been bound for years to a single supplier with few exit routes — which it described as an open door to cost overruns.

Two independent reviews, in a single year, reaching the same structural conclusion from opposite ends. One looked across eleven projects and found systemic gaps. The other looked deeply into one project and found a decade of concealment and a procurement structure built to drift. They describe the same machine.

## The public cost

The total public cost extends beyond any single overrun. A state that loses the ability to build and oversee its own technology pays the same premium again and again — to outside vendors, on projects the public struggles to follow and cannot easily price. Citizens absorb service failures when systems launch before they are ready. And accountability erodes slowly, because when responsibility is spread across agencies and consultants, no single person answers for the result.

## What must change

The remedies are not mysterious, and they are not new. They were set out after Charbonneau and proven in other jurisdictions. They begin with the AMP's own first recommendation: strengthen central governance of public digital projects and place it where it can shape decisions before the money is committed; rebuild and use the state's own expertise rather than defaulting to outside suppliers. They extend to genuine protection for those who warn from inside, to procurement that preserves competition, and to accountability that does not evaporate when a project fails.

The authority to make these changes sits with the government and the Treasury Board. The recommendations are already on the public record. The open question is implementation.

## Why now

A general election is set for October 5, 2026. Several oversight processes touching these failures remain active, including a continuing criminal investigation arising from SAAQclic and audits still underway in the health technology file that [Part Three](/en/dossiers/cout-invisible-partie-3/) examines. The reports have been written. The evidence is public. What remains is whether the people with the authority to act will be asked, on the record and before the vote, what they intend to do.
